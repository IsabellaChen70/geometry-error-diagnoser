## Sub-topic 1: AI/VLM Failures on Composed Geometric Transformations (sequences of rotate/reflect/translate)

### Per-paper summaries

**1. Visual Reasoning Benchmark (VRB)**
- **Citation:** Mohamed Huti, Alasdair Mackintosh, Amy Waldock, Dominic Andrews, Maxime Lelièvre, Moritz Boos, Tobias Murray, Paul Atherton, Robin A. A. Ince, Oliver G. B. Garrod. "Visual Reasoning Benchmark: Evaluating Multimodal LLMs on Classroom-Authentic Visual Problems from Primary Education." arXiv:2602.12196, Feb 12, 2026. Preprint, no conference venue found.
- **Institution/experts:** Fab AI (all ten authors; industry lab, not an academic group with prior track record in this niche).
- **Summary:** Built from 701 real, unedited exam questions from Zambian Grade 7 and Indian Class 6 national exams, using minimal-text "classroom-authentic" images rather than synthetic diagrams. Covers non-verbal reasoning types spanning static skills (counting, scaling, pattern completion) and dynamic/transformation skills (folding, reflection, rotation). 45 models evaluated against a human/expert reliability threshold from three independent adult annotators.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Accuracy ranged near-chance to 78% (best: Gemini-3.0 Flash; best open-weight: Kimi-K2.5 at 60%) vs. 94% human inter-annotator agreement. Marginal effects on accuracy: Folding −9 pts (hardest), Reflection −5.8 pts, Rotation −5 pts, versus positive effects for static skills (Scaling +5 pts, Counting +1.4 pts). The paper characterizes this as a **"spatial ceiling"** and a **"jagged frontier."**

**2. Mind's Eye: A Benchmark of Visual Abstraction, Transformation and Composition for Multimodal LLMs**
- **Citation:** Rohit Sinha, Aditya Kanade, Sai Srinivas Kancheti, Vineeth N Balasubramanian, Tanuja Ganu. arXiv:2604.16054, April 17, 2026 (v1). Preprint.
- **Institution/experts:** IIT Hyderabad (Sinha, Kancheti — CSE Dept.) and Microsoft Research, Bengaluru (Kanade, Balasubramanian, Ganu).
- **Summary:** The single most directly on-target paper in this sub-topic. Introduces an 8-task, multiple-choice "A-R-T" (Abstraction-Relation-Transformation) taxonomy where the Transformation axis explicitly separates single-operation Mental Transformation (MT) from Paper Folding (PF, forward tracking + mental reversal) and — critically — **Mental Composition (MC)**, a direct test of chained/sequential transformations, evaluated against a human baseline.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Human baseline: MT 81%, PF 80.1%, MC 82% (humans handle single and composed transformations almost identically). Models: MT 10.1–35.6%, PF 11.3–32.0%, MC 13.0–51.8% — far below humans with wide variance on MC. Quote: "models particularly struggle with tasks requiring interpreting temporal sequences and tracking visual elements across transformations," and "Paper Folding requires not only forward tracking but also mentally reversing the process."
- *(Note: distinct from the earlier, differently-scoped "Mind's Eye of LLMs: Visualization-of-Thought..." by Wu et al., arXiv:2404.03622, NeurIPS 2024, Microsoft Research — that paper proposes a prompting method for navigation/grid tasks and reports no rotation/reflection accuracy numbers. UNVERIFIED as directly relevant beyond its "atomic transformation decomposition" framing.)*

**3. Seeing Isn't Orienting (DORI benchmark)**
- **Citation:** Nazia Tasnim, Keanu Nichols, Yuting Yan, Nicholas Ikechukwu, Elva Zou, Deepti Ghadiyaram, Bryan A. Plummer. "Seeing Isn't Orienting: A Cognitively Grounded Benchmark Reveals Systematic Orientation Failures in MLLMs." arXiv:2505.21649, May 27, 2025 (revised through v7, April 2026). A related/duplicate-titled posting exists at arXiv:2603.11410 — UNVERIFIED why two IDs exist for overlapping content.
- **Institution/experts:** Boston University (primary — Tasnim, Nichols, Ikechukwu, Zou, Ghadiyaram, Plummer), Johns Hopkins University (Yuting Yan), Runway (one co-author). Plummer and Ghadiyaram are repeat contributors to spatial-reasoning benchmarking (e.g., Plummer co-authored R2D3).
- **Summary:** Introduces DORI (Discriminative Orientation Reasoning Intelligence), decomposing orientation understanding into four dimensions — frontal alignment, rotational transformations (single-axis **and compound**), relative directional relationships, canonical orientation — tested at coarse and granular precision. 13,652 images, 14 sources, 33,656 MC questions, 24 VLMs evaluated with an expert human baseline (Table 9).
- **Key facts/quotes:** Best models overall: 54.2% coarse, 45.0%/33.0% granular (discrepancy across abstract vs. body flagged as worth independent verification, likely v1 vs v7 revisions). Single-axis rotation: human 92% coarse/82% granular vs. top open-source models ~49.0% coarse. **Compound rotation:** human 86% coarse/86% granular (only a 6-point human gap between single and compound) vs. models "collapse dramatically" — best open-source ~49.0% coarse, closed-source granular compound as low as **8.6%**. Quote: "models competent on general spatial benchmarks remain near-random on object-centric orientation tasks," with "the largest drops on compound rotations," and performance "drops by 30% on dynamic rotational tasks relative to static pose identification."

**4. GeoEval (verified NOT about transformation composition)**
- **Citation:** Jiaxin Zhang, Zhongzhi Li, Mingliang Zhang, Fei Yin, Chenglin Liu, Yashar Moshfeghi. "GeoEval: Benchmark for Evaluating LLMs and Multi-Modal Models on Geometry Problem-Solving." arXiv:2402.10104, Feb 15, 2024 (v2 May 2024). ACL 2024 Findings.
- **Institution/experts:** School of Artificial Intelligence, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; MAIS, Institute of Automation, CAS; Dept. of Computer & Information Sciences, University of Strathclyde (Moshfeghi).
- **Summary:** 2,000 main + 750 backward-reasoning + 2,000 augmented + 300 "hard" problems spanning flat/solid/analytic geometry (calculate/prove/judge types). Direct examination confirmed **no dedicated subset isolating rotation/reflection/translation/composition** — this is static proof/calculation geometry, not transformation-sequence reasoning.
- **Key facts/quotes:** WizardMath: 55.67% main subset, only 6.00% hard subset. GPT-series models scored notably better on self-rephrased problem versions (a robustness red flag). **Scope correction: treat as background/contrast context only, not on-topic for composed-transformation reasoning.**

**5. GeoLaux (verified NOT about transformation composition)**
- **Citation:** Yumeng Fu, Jiayin Zhu, Lingling Zhang, Wenjun Wu, Bo Zhao, Shaoxuan Ma, Yushun Zhang, Jun Liu. "GeoLaux: A Benchmark for Evaluating MLLMs' Geometry Performance on Long-Step Problems Requiring Auxiliary Lines." arXiv:2508.06226, Aug 8, 2025 (v4 May 2026).
- **Institution/experts:** Xi'an Jiaotong University (School of Computer Science and Technology; Ministry of Education Key Laboratory of Intelligent Networks and Network Security; School of Software Engineering) — all authors.
- **Summary:** 2,186 calculation/proof problems with long solution chains (mean 6.51 steps, max 24), 41.8% requiring auxiliary-line construction. 23 MLLMs evaluated on process- vs. answer-level correctness. "Long steps" refers to auxiliary-line/algebraic proof steps, **not** rigid-motion transformation sequences.
- **Key facts/quotes:** 18 of 23 models showed a **>50% performance drop** moving from short-step to long-step problems. "Limited answer hints effectively improve process correctness, whereas explicit answers lead models to neglect intermediate reasoning steps." Relevant only as an analogous (multi-step reasoning degrades sharply) finding, not a transformation-composition benchmark.

**6. Geometry3K / UniGeo / GeoQA / PGPS9K / MathVista cluster (verified largely NOT transformation-composition benchmarks)**
- **Citations:** Geometry3K (Lu et al.); UniGeo; GeoQA/GeoQA+; PGPS9K; MathVista (Lu et al., arXiv:2310.02255); survey: "Towards Geometry Problem Solving in the Large Model Era," arXiv:2506.02690.
- **Institution/experts:** Multiple (see Sub-topic 2 for Geometry3K/Inter-GPS authorship detail).
- **Summary:** These five are the most-cited diagram-based geometry benchmarks. Verified to focus almost exclusively on static plane geometry (angles, lengths, areas, congruence/similarity proofs), "with little or no coverage of 3D reasoning" per the 2025 survey. MathVista aggregates 28 existing + 3 new datasets (6,141 examples); its solid-geometry content is limited to ~62 basic object-counting problems (per SolidGeo's critique) and it does not isolate sequential transformation reasoning.
- **Key facts/quotes:** MathVista: GPT-4V 49.9%, Gemini 50.3% overall; GPT-4V "surpassing human performance... in geometry diagrams" on some subsets yet "10.4% short of human performance" overall. **Conclusion: UNVERIFIED/not applicable to composed-transformation reasoning** — flagged explicitly rather than stretched.

**7. TangramPuzzle**
- **Citation:** Daixian Liu, Jiayi Kuang, Yinghui Li, Yangning Li, Di Yin, Haoyu Cao, Xing Sun, Ying Shen, Hai-Tao Zheng, Liang Lin, Philip S. Yu. "TangramPuzzle: Evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models with Compositional Spatial Reasoning." arXiv:2601.16520, Jan 23, 2026. Preprint.
- **Institution/experts:** Tsinghua University, Sun Yat-Sen University, Tencent Youtu Lab, University of Illinois Chicago (Philip S. Yu).
- **Summary:** Uses the classic tangram puzzle (7 pieces assembled via rotation/reflection/translation into a target silhouette) as a geometry-grounded compositional reasoning test. Introduces "Tangram Construction Expression" (TCE), a symbolic/machine-verifiable coordinate encoding to avoid ambiguous visual grading. Two tasks: Outline Prediction (infer shape from local placements) and End-to-End Code Generation (generate exact transformation placements — a direct test of composing multiple transformations correctly).
- **Key facts/quotes:** Outline Prediction: Gemini3-Pro 98.65%, GPT-5.2 77.54%, Qwen3-VL-32B 73.05%, Claude-Sonnet-4.5 51.20%, GLM-4.6V 30.84%, InternVL3-78B 29.19%, DeepSeek-VL2 25.45%, DeepSeek-OCR 22.46%. **End-to-End (full composition) success:** Gemini3-Pro 22.60%, Claude-Sonnet-4.5 0.15%, GPT-5.2 0%, essentially all others 0–0.15%. Human baseline (Task 2): 72.67% (range 46–100%). The gap between outline-recognition (many models >50%) and full compositional generation (nearly all models ≈0%) is the starkest single number in this entire literature map. Quote: "MLLMs tend to prioritize matching the target silhouette while neglecting geometric constraints"; humans showed "a near-binary pattern: solutions are either successfully completed or not solved at all."

**8. OrigamiSpace**
- **Citation:** Rui Xu, Dakuan Lu, Zicheng Zhao, Xiaoyu Tan, Xintao Wang, Siyu Yuan, Jiangjie Chen, Yinghui Xu. "ORIGAMISPACE: Benchmarking Multimodal LLMs in Multi-Step Spatial Reasoning with Mathematical Constraints." arXiv:2511.18450, Nov 23, 2025. NeurIPS 2025 (poster).
- **Institution/experts:** Fudan University, SII (Shanghai Innovation Institute — full expansion UNVERIFIED), INF Technology.
- **Summary:** 350 origami instances (crease-pattern diagram, compiled flat pattern, full folding process, final shape). Four tasks including Multi-step Spatial Reasoning; folding = sequence of ordered transformations (each fold = a reflection about a crease line), "each step depends on the result of the previous one." Sequence lengths 3–25 steps (mean 8.2).
- **Key facts/quotes:** Non-expert human baseline: 51.18%; expert human: 100.00%. Best models: Gemini 2.5-Pro 53.45% (±0.74), GPT-4o 51.81% (±0.48) — roughly matching non-expert humans, far below experts. Worst: LLaVA-1.5-7B 29.05% (±1.90), DeepSeek-VL2 30.01% (±0.06). Quote: origami "involv[es] a sequence of ordered folding operations where each step depends on the result of the previous one, embodying the essence of multi-step reasoning."

**9. LEGO-Puzzles**
- **Citation:** Kexian Tang, Junyao Gao, Yanhong Zeng, Haodong Duan, Yanan Sun, Zhening Xing, Wenran Liu, Kaifeng Lyu, Kai Chen. "LEGO-Puzzles: How Good Are MLLMs at Multi-Step Spatial Reasoning?" arXiv:2503.19990, March 25, 2025 (v3 June 2025).
- **Institution/experts:** Shanghai AI Laboratory (Zeng, Duan, corresponding author Kai Chen), Tongji University (Tang, Gao), Tsinghua University (Lyu, corresponding author).
- **Summary:** 1,100+ VQA items across 11 tasks on LEGO brick assembly, including an explicit "Ordering" task (sequence correct assembly steps) and a "Backwards" task (identify valid intermediate states given start/end states — directly analogous to inferring a two-step transformation sequence mapping shape A to B). Tested chain-of-thought prompting as mitigation against a human baseline.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Human baseline: 93.6% overall. Top models: GPT-4o 59.1%, Gemini-2.0-Flash 55.5%, Claude-3.5-Sonnet 54.1% (paper's own framing: "only about half"). Ordering and Backwards tasks showed sharpest degradation, multiple models at **near-zero accuracy** on Ordering. Chain-of-thought gave only "limited help," with benefit diminishing "as task complexity increased beyond two steps." Quote: reasoning prompts provided "limited help in multi-step spatial reasoning," with several models reaching "near-zero accuracy" on step-ordering.

- **Citation:** OpenReview id `up2LD7vVdW`, apparently associated with ICLR 2026 based on venue calendar. **UNVERIFIED: authors and institution** — appears to be (or was) an anonymous double-blind submission; signals suggest possible withdrawal from ICLR 2026. - **Institution/experts:** UNVERIFIED (anonymous submission).
- **Summary (secondhand, requires independent verification):** Reportedly evaluates spatial transformation reasoning across 2D shapes, 3D mental rotation, and multi-object scenes, distinguishing "state prediction" (predict outcome after a transformation/sequence) from "transformation inference" (recover the operation(s) from before/after states — precisely the "identify the two-step sequence mapping shape A to shape B" framing).

### Cross-cutting observation (not a distinct paper, but part of the raw research)
No single lab has multiple first-author papers narrowly on shape-transformation composition specifically, but Shanghai AI Laboratory (LEGO-Puzzles), Boston University (DORI), and Microsoft Research Bengaluru + IIT Hyderabad (Mind's Eye) are repeat contributors to the adjacent "compositional/multi-step spatial reasoning in MLLMs" niche. This remains a young, fragmented sub-area as of mid-2026 — no individual researcher has 3+ papers specifically on composed transformation sequencing.

### DOK1 — Facts

- VRB: best model 78% (Gemini-3.0 Flash) vs. 94% human agreement; Folding −9 pts, Reflection −5.8 pts, Rotation −5 pts marginal accuracy effects (vs. positive effects for static skills).
- Mind's Eye: human MT 81%/PF 80.1%/MC 82% vs. models MT 10.1–35.6%/PF 11.3–32.0%/MC 13.0–51.8%.
- DORI: human single-axis rotation 92% coarse/82% granular vs. models ~49.0% coarse; human compound rotation 86%/86% (only 6-pt human penalty) vs. models collapsing to as low as 8.6% granular compound; overall models "drop by 30% on dynamic rotational tasks relative to static pose identification."
- GeoEval: WizardMath 55.67% (main) vs. 6.00% (hard subset) — but NOT a transformation-composition benchmark (verified negative).
- GeoLaux: 18/23 models show >50% performance drop from short-step to long-step problems — but steps are auxiliary-line/proof steps, not rigid-motion sequences (verified negative).
- MathVista: GPT-4V 49.9%, Gemini 50.3% overall — no rotation/reflection/composition subtask breakdown exists (verified negative for this sub-topic).
- TangramPuzzle: Outline Prediction up to 98.65% (Gemini3-Pro) but End-to-End full-composition success collapses to 22.60% (best model) and 0–0.15% for most others, vs. 72.67% human baseline — the single starkest recognition-vs-composition gap found.
- OrigamiSpace: non-expert human 51.18%, expert human 100.00%, best models ~51.8–53.45% (matching non-experts only), worst models ~29–30%.
- LEGO-Puzzles: human 93.6% vs. top models ~54–59%; Ordering/Backwards tasks show near-zero accuracy for several models; chain-of-thought benefit "diminishes as task complexity increases beyond two steps."
- TransformEval/"Rotate in Mind?": UNVERIFIED numbers; UNVERIFIED authorship — reported qualitative claim only (models better at inferring transformations than predicting outcomes; fail at ordering and translation).

### DOK2 — Concepts

**Pattern across papers:** Every benchmark that separately measures single-transformation vs. composed/sequential/compound transformation performance (Mind's Eye, DORI, LEGO-Puzzles, TangramPuzzle, OrigamiSpace) finds the same qualitative shape: humans show a small or negligible penalty for composition (DORI: only 6 pts; Mind's Eye: MT/PF/MC all ~80-82%), while models show a severe, sometimes near-total collapse specifically on the composed/sequential/ordering condition, not on single-operation recognition. TangramPuzzle's outline-recognition-vs-full-composition gap (up to 98% down to ~0%) is the most extreme illustration. This is a genuinely consistent pattern across five independently-run benchmarks from different labs (IIT Hyderabad/MSR, Boston University, Shanghai AI Lab, Tsinghua/SYSU/Tencent, Fudan/SII), which is meaningfully stronger evidence than any single paper alone.

**Evidence strength, honestly assessed:** Strong for the general claim "composition is disproportionately harder for models than for humans, relative to single-step operations." Weaker for anything specific to the "rotate then reflect" or "translate then rotate" framing named in the project brief — none of the verified benchmarks test exactly two sequential *rigid motions on a labeled 2D shape* in the precise way a typical school geometry problem would (tangrams, LEGO, and origami are structurally analogous but not identical domains). GeoEval, GeoLaux, and the Geometry3K/MathVista cluster were explicitly checked and ruled out as irrelevant — a useful negative result showing that the *mainstream* geometry-QA benchmark literature does not test this at all, leaving only the more tangential puzzle/spatial-cognition benchmarks as evidence. The single closest-matching paper (TransformEval) is UNVERIFIED at every level (authorship, numbers, even venue status) and should not be leaned on.

**Implications for this project's design:** This sub-topic is about *model* capability on composed transformations, not directly about the "diagram→text→solve→diagnose" pipeline question — but it has one important second-order implication: if the base/target model used inside the pipeline is itself weak at composed-transformation reasoning (as this literature strongly suggests generically), then the "solve from text" stage of the pipeline needs a solving approach that does not rely on the model's own spatial/visual composition intuition — supporting a symbolic or coordinate-based solving step downstream of the diagram-to-text conversion, rather than expecting a VLM to reason end-to-end over composed motions from pixels. It also suggests that a diagnoser trained to spot *composition-specific* misconceptions (e.g., failing to update the reference frame for the second transformation) is targeting a real and measurable capability gap, not a strawman — the gap is consistently reproduced across independent benchmarks, even if none of them map perfectly onto the school-geometry-problem format this project targets.

## Sub-topic 2: Converting Geometric Diagrams to Text/Symbolic Representations (diagram-to-text grounding)

### Per-paper summaries

**1. "Can Large Language Models Integrate Spatial Data?"**
- **Citation:** Bin Han, Robert Wolfe, Anat Caspi, Bill Howe. "Can Large Language Models Integrate Spatial Data? Empirical Insights into Reasoning Strengths and Computational Weaknesses." arXiv:2508.05009v1, August 2025.
- **Institution/experts:** University of Washington, Seattle.
- **Summary:** Tests LLMs on spatial-join and spatial-union tasks over real GeoJSON linestring data (sidewalks/roads from Bellevue, WA), running a direct ablation isolating "raw geometry description in text → reason" vs. "pre-computed geometric features → reason," plus a separate vision-language (image) vs. text-only condition. This is the cleanest ablation found addressing whether decomposition helps, though about spatial join/union rather than classic diagram geometry.
- **Key facts/quotes (high confidence, read directly from PDF):** Plain natural-language prompting: only 54.2%/57.5% (zero/few-shot) accuracy on spatial join, ~39% below heuristic baselines. Pre-computed geometric features (min-angle, min-distance, overlap-area) raised accuracy to 90–98.4% (qwen-plus, GPT-4o), matching/beating rule-based heuristics. For GPT-4o, spatial join: plain-text 58.0% → image 72.8% → structured-features 94.8%; spatial union: plain-text 81.7% → image 88.9% → structured-features 95.5%. Quote: "heuristic prompting using geometric features (features) still outperforms prompting with visual inputs... explicitly providing geometric features remains more effective for these tasks." On failure modes: "models are ineffective at computational geometry... Errors fall into three categories: (1) Incorrect computational logic... (2) Unclear computational explanations... (3) Conclusions without computation."

**2. Inter-GPS**
- **Citation:** Pan Lu, Ran Gong, Shibiao Jiang, Liang Qiu, Siyuan Huang, Xiaodan Liang, Song-Chun Zhu. "Inter-GPS: Interpretable Geometry Problem Solving with Formal Language and Symbolic Reasoning." ACL-IJCNLP 2021. arXiv:2105.04165.
- **Institution/experts:** Center for Vision, Cognition, Learning, and Autonomy (VCLA), UCLA; College of Computer Science and Technology, Zhejiang University; School of Intelligent Systems Engineering, Sun Yat-sen University.
- **Summary:** Parses geometry problem text (rule-based) and the diagram (neural parser: Hough Transformation for primitives, RetinaNet for symbol/text regions, MathPix OCR for text) into a shared formal language of logic forms, then applies a symbolic theorem-prover with predicted theorem sequencing. Also introduces the **Geometry3K** dataset (3,002 problems with dense formal-language annotations: 2,401 train/300 val/601 test; 6,293 text literals, 27,213 diagram literals).
- **Key facts/quotes:** Overall solving accuracy on Geometry3K: **57.5%**, confirmed via two independent secondary sources, consistently described as "a significant improvement over existing methods." **UNVERIFIED:** the specific diagram-parser-only accuracy (per-relation-type extraction, Table 5/19–24) and a clean before/after ablation isolating the diagram-parsing step's contribution — PDF table extraction failed (tables likely rendered as images). Secondary summaries state qualitatively that "parsing accuracy and theorem selection are critical to the solver's success," but exact figures are UNVERIFIED. GEOS dataset comparison numbers: also UNVERIFIED.

**3. PGDP5K + PGDPNet**
- **Citation:** Yihan Hao, Ming-Liang Zhang, Fei Yin, Linlin Huang. "PGDP5K: A Diagram Parsing Dataset for Plane Geometry Problems." ICPR 2022 / arXiv:2205.09947. Companion: Ming-Liang Zhang, Fei Yin, Yi-Han Hao, Cheng-Lin Liu. "Plane Geometry Diagram Parsing" (proposes PGDPNet). IJCAI 2022. arXiv:2205.09363.
- **Institution/experts:** Yihan Hao and Linlin Huang — Beijing Jiaotong University; Ming-Liang Zhang, Fei Yin, Yi-Han Hao — Institute of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences (also UCAS). Cheng-Lin Liu — Institute of Automation, CAS (well-known NLPR/CASIA researcher; affiliation high-confidence but not independently reconfirmed).
- **Summary:** PGDP5K is a 5,000-diagram dataset for plane-geometry diagram parsing with primitive-level annotation (16 shape types, 5 positional relations, 22 symbol types, 6 text types), designed to isolate the diagram→structured-representation step from downstream solving. PGDPNet is an end-to-end neural model (modified instance segmentation + GNN) for this parsing task, benchmarked against the Inter-GPS diagram parser as baseline.
- **Key facts/quotes:** State-of-the-art diagram parsing on PGDP5K/IMP-Geometry3K achieves only **66.07% F1** (direct quote from abstract, high confidence) — even the best diagram parser gets roughly a third of diagram elements/relations wrong. PGDPNet improves the "Totally Same of All" (fully-correct-parse) metric by 57.2%/57.4% relative to the Inter-GPS baseline — **UNVERIFIED** (could not confirm if relative-% or percentage-point figure; ambiguous phrasing). Separately reported (possibly a different, follow-on paper): match accuracy 91.51%, perfect accuracy 56.47%, end-to-end problem-solving 63.45% — **UNVERIFIED**, could not confirm source paper/table.

**4. GeoDRL**
- **Citation:** Shuai Peng, Di Fu, Yijun Liang, Liangcai Gao, Zhi Tang. "GeoDRL: A Self-Learning Framework for Geometry Problem Solving using Reinforcement Learning in Deductive Reasoning." Findings of ACL 2023.
- **Institution/experts:** Peking University (Gao and Peng confirmed via ResearchGate — Wangxuan Institute of Computer Technology); Zhi Tang also a well-known PKU researcher. Di Fu and Yijun Liang's affiliations: UNVERIFIED.
- **Summary:** Explicitly separates the "perceive/represent" step (building a **Geometry Logic Graph**, a symbolic graph representation of the diagram's entities/relations) from the "reason" step (a GNN + Deep RL agent performing deductive reasoning as sequential decision-making over that graph, rather than brute-force symbolic search) — a clean example of a "perceive vs. reason" separation architecture.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Improves accuracy on Geometry3K by 11.1% over previous SOTA — repeated consistently across searches but **UNVERIFIED** against primary PDF table. Achieved via unsupervised self-learning (RL), improving efficiency/interpretability over symbolic-search baselines (abstract-level claim).

**5. GeoQA / NGS**
- **Citation:** Jiaqi Chen, Jianheng Tang, Jinghui Qin, Xiaodan Liang, Lingbo Liu, Eric P. Xing, Liang Lin. "GeoQA: A Geometric Question Answering Benchmark Towards Multimodal Numerical Reasoning." Findings of ACL-IJCNLP 2021.
- **Institution/experts:** UNVERIFIED precisely, but based on commonly-reported author affiliations elsewhere: likely Sun Yat-sen University (Liang, Lin) / CMU-MBZUAI (Xing) collaboration — not independently confirmed.
- **Summary:** Introduces both the GeoQA benchmark and the NGS (Neural Geometric Solver) baseline — "the first neural geometry solver," an encoder-decoder architecture encoding problem text and diagram separately, fusing via a multimodal fusion module, then decoding into an executable operation-sequence program. This is a "joint-but-separable" perceive/reason split: separate per-modality encoding, but fusion/reasoning learned jointly rather than via an explicit intermediate symbolic/textual representation.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Follow-on works noted for trajectory context (each UNVERIFIED individually beyond what's stated): DPE-NGS (2022, added Bi-LSTM+RoBERTa text encoding); SCA-GPS (2023, "Symbolic Character-Aware," arXiv:2308.02823); Geoformer (T5-backbone, added geometry-proving as auxiliary task).

**6. GOLD**
- **Citation:** Jiaxin Zhang, Yashar Moshfeghi. "GOLD: Geometry Problem Solver with Natural Language Description." Findings of NAACL 2024.
- **Institution/experts:** University of Strathclyde (confirmed via Strathclyde PureData/Strathprints repository).
- **Summary:** The paper most directly on-point for the "diagram→text→reason" pipeline question applied to classic geometry diagrams specifically (not just spatial joins). Explicitly extracts geometric primitives/symbols from the diagram, converts them into natural-language descriptions, then feeds this text into an LLM to generate a solution program.
- **Key facts/quotes:** On UniGeo: 12.7% accuracy improvement on the calculation subset and 42.1% on the proving subset over the previous best model (Geoformer) — **UNVERIFIED at primary-source level** (could not confirm percentage-point vs. relative-percentage interpretation). The paper does not appear, per available summaries, to report a controlled ablation isolating "text-description pipeline" vs. "same model with direct image input" holding everything else fixed — flagged as something to verify directly if this becomes central to the project's argument.

**7. Levental — "Can Vision-Language Models See Squares?" — FLAGGED, LOW RELIABILITY**
- **Citation:** Yuval Levental. "Can Vision-Language Models See Squares? Text-Recognition Mediates Spatial Reasoning Across Three Model Families." arXiv:2602.15950v2, Feb 2026.
- **Institution/experts:** Rochester Institute of Technology (sole author, student-level, no institutional co-authorship or lab backing).
- **Summary:** Reliability caveat (explicit, important): evaluates "Claude Opus (claude-opus-4-6)," "ChatGPT 5.2," "Gemini 3 Thinking" via casual web chat interfaces (not APIs), with no error bars, a single trial per condition, and author-acknowledged high run-to-run variance without repeated trials. Has hallmarks of a rapid/non-rigorous (possibly AI-assisted) preprint rather than a vetted contribution. Included only because it is topically relevant to diagram-misreading failure modes; every number should be treated as **UNVERIFIED/low-confidence** pending replication or peer review.
- **Key facts/quotes (UNVERIFIED):** On text-symbol-rendered grids (`.`/`#`), Claude/ChatGPT ~91% cell accuracy, ~83–84% F1; on visually-identical filled-square grids (no text), all three collapsed to 60–73% accuracy, 29–39% F1 — a 34–54 point F1 gap attributed to reliance on an internal "OCR/text-recognition pathway." Distinct failure modes: Claude under-counts filled cells (45 predicted vs. 60 actual) and mislocalizes boundaries; ChatGPT over-counts dramatically (112–133 vs. 78–94 actual) and loses grid dimensions; Gemini exhibits "template hallucination" (abandons input, generates stereotyped patterns).

**8. Additional papers found but not deeply verified**
- "Unleashing Spatial Reasoning in Multimodal Large Language Models via Textual Representation Guided Reasoning" (arXiv:2603.23404) — claims a text-based spatial intermediate representation improves MLLM spatial reasoning; full ablation numbers **UNVERIFIED** (fetch failed due to file size).
- A WebSearch-only claim (source untraceable): "When geometric diagrams are fully described in formal language, reasoning in pure text space holds an advantage over multimodal reasoning," and video-to-text conversion "nearly doubles" predictions within 20% error — **UNVERIFIED**, could not pin down origin paper (possibly GeoX or the spatial-data-integration paper).
- **GeoX** — Xia, Li, Ye, Wu, Zhou, Yuan, Peng, Cai, Yan, Wang, He, Shi, Chen, Yan, Zhang. Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai AI Laboratory, Fudan University, MMLab@CUHK. arXiv:2412.11863. Uses a dedicated pre-trained diagram encoder ("Geo-ViT," masked-autoencoder-pretrained on 120K+ diagrams) plus a symbol decoder, unified formalized vision-language pretraining — a contrasting "don't decompose to text, pretrain a better diagram encoder instead" approach. ### DOK1 — Facts

- Han et al.: plain-text spatial-join accuracy 54.2–58.0%; structured geometric features raise this to 90–98.4%; images alone (72.8–88.9%) outperform plain text but underperform structured features (94.8–95.5%).

- PGDP5K/PGDPNet: SOTA diagram parsing F1 only 66.07% — diagram parsing remains a hard, error-prone bottleneck even with a dedicated fine-grained dataset.
- GeoDRL: reported 11.1% accuracy improvement over prior SOTA on Geometry3K via a perceive-then-reason (Geometry Logic Graph + GNN/RL) architecture — UNVERIFIED against primary table.
- GOLD: reported 12.7% (calculation) / 42.1% (proving) improvement over Geoformer on UniGeo via a diagram→NL-description→LLM pipeline — UNVERIFIED (percentage-point vs. relative ambiguity), and no controlled text-vs-image ablation reported.
- Levental (low-reliability source): text-symbol grids ~91% accuracy/~83-84% F1 vs. non-textual filled-square grids 60-73% accuracy/29-39% F1 — all UNVERIFIED/low-confidence.
- GeoX (contrasting approach): 54.9% (GeoQA), 54.4% (UniGeo), 58.6% (Geometry3K), 52.7% (PGPS9K) via pretrained diagram encoder rather than text decomposition — UNVERIFIED at exact-number level.

### DOK2 — Concepts

**Pattern across papers:** Two convergent signals recur: (1) raw/naive text or plain natural-language description of a diagram underperforms both images and structured/symbolic representations (Han et al.'s clean three-way ablation is the strongest evidence here); (2) even when explicit diagram-parsing-to-symbolic-form is attempted by dedicated systems (Inter-GPS, PGDP5K/PGDPNet), the parsing step itself is a substantial, quantified bottleneck — the best diagram parser is only 66.07% F1, meaning roughly a third of diagram content is mis-extracted before reasoning even begins.

**Evidence strength, honestly assessed:** The strongest, most rigorously verified single data point is Han et al.'s ablation (spatial join/union, not classroom geometry diagrams — an adjacent domain, not a direct hit). The most topically relevant paper (GOLD, diagram→NL→LLM for actual geometry problems) has UNVERIFIED numbers and, importantly, does NOT report the controlled ablation that would most directly settle the question (same model, text-pipeline vs. direct-image-input). Inter-GPS's foundational 57.5% figure is solid, but the specific diagram-parsing-contribution ablation could not be extracted from any source consulted. The PGDP5K 66.07% F1 figure is the single most load-bearing, well-verified number for this project because it directly quantifies the risk in the proposed pipeline's first stage. The Levental paper's failure-mode taxonomy (under-counting, over-counting, template hallucination) is a useful hypothesis-generator but not citable as quantitative evidence.

**Implications for this project's design:** This sub-topic gives genuine, if incomplete, support for a diagram→text pipeline — but with an important caveat directly relevant to the project's architecture: the text/symbolic conversion step itself is not free of error (66.07% F1 best case in a dedicated, fine-tuned parser). For a diagnoser whose whole premise depends on first getting a faithful text/symbolic description of the diagram, this means diagram-to-text conversion should be treated as its own source of diagnostic error, not just a clean preprocessing step — the pipeline may need either (a) a validation/self-check step after diagram-to-text conversion, (b) a diagram-to-text component specifically fine-tuned on the target problem style (composed transformations), since none of the verified diagram parsers here were built or tested for transformation-sequence diagrams specifically, or (c) awareness that misconceptions inferred by the downstream diagnoser could actually originate in diagram-parsing errors rather than genuine student/model reasoning errors — a confound worth explicitly flagging in the project.

## Sub-topic 3: Student Misconceptions Specifically About Composed Geometric Transformations

### Per-paper summaries

**1. Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly (2017)**
- **Citation:** Hegg, M., & Fukawa-Connelly, T. "Student Proficiency with Transformational Geometry After a College Proof-Based Geometry Class." Conference on Research in Undergraduate Mathematics Education (CRUME/RUME) Proceedings, pp. 1–9 (contributed report). **Verified — primary source, full text obtained and read directly.**
- **Institution/experts:** Not stated in raw research beyond venue; RUME conference contributors (moderate-to-solid rigor venue, standard for in-progress math-education research, not as vetted as JRME/ESM).
- **Summary:** The single strongest, most directly on-point source for composition-specific misconceptions. In a final-exam item, students were asked whether an isometry R₁ mapping one triangle onto another was *unique*. A cluster of incorrect reasoning revealed that students believed an isometry can only be "one move," rather than recognizing that a composition of transformations is itself a single transformation — directly naming the conflation this project cares about.
- **Key facts/quotes (exact, from primary text):** Of 17 preservice-teacher participants: 9 said not unique, 8 said unique, but only 4 gave a correct justification. Of incorrect justifications, "Seven students gave a response that indicated that their understanding of unique implies that only one 'move' can be used (ignoring the idea that a composition of transformations is a transformation)... These students wrote claims such as, 'Yes because any other isometry would require more than one isometry.'" **Caveat:** small sample (N=17), preservice teachers (not K-12), conference contributed report rather than peer-reviewed journal article.

**2. Edwards (1997)**
- **Citation:** Edwards, L. D. "Exploring the Territory Before Proof: Students' Generalizations in a Computer Microworld for Transformation Geometry." *International Journal of Computers for Mathematical Learning*, 2(3), 187–215. Citation corroborated across multiple independent sources; full text paywalled — content below is **UNVERIFIED beyond abstract-level description.**
- **Institution/experts:** Not stated in raw research (peer-reviewed journal, well-regarded in math-ed technology research).
- **Summary:** Per secondary description, reports on middle/high school students using a Logo-based microworld specifically to investigate the **composition of two reflections** — the canonical case for discovering that two reflections over intersecting lines yield a rotation, and over parallel lines yield a translation. Reportedly documents students' informal conjectures and reasoning paths before formal proof, with a detailed case study of one student pair.
- **Key facts/quotes:** No verbatim quote about specific errors could be obtained. **Flag: UNVERIFIED for specific error content; VERIFIED only that the paper's topic is composition of reflections and student generalization/proof-readiness.**

**3. Portnoy, Grundmeier, & Graham (2006)**
- **Citation:** Portnoy, N., Grundmeier, T. A., & Graham, K. J. "Students' Understanding of Mathematical Objects in the Context of Transformational Geometry: Implications for Constructing and Understanding Proofs." *The Journal of Mathematical Behavior*, 25(3), 196–207. Corroborated via multiple independent secondary sources including verbatim description embedded in the verified Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly primary text; original text not independently obtained.
- **Institution/experts:** Not stated in raw research (peer-reviewed journal).
- **Summary:** Study of prospective secondary teachers found students held a **process conception** of transformations (focusing on the "move" itself) rather than an **object conception** (treating the transformation's output as a mathematical object that can itself be operated on). An object conception is a prerequisite for treating "transformation A followed by transformation B" as a single well-defined object that can be reasoned about and proven equivalent to a third transformation. Not composition-*specific* but theoretically foundational to why composition proofs are hard.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Quantitative data UNVERIFIED — secondary sources describe the finding only qualitatively ("most of the students... had a process view... as a result, they had difficulty..."). Direct quote (as reproduced within the verified Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly text): "...students' conceptions of geometric objects as perceived objects and their process conceptions of transformations hindered their abilities to [construct proofs]." **Caveat:** do not cite as direct evidence of, e.g., commutativity errors — it isn't that; it's about conception type.

**4. Fife, James, & Bauer (2019)**
- **Citation:** Fife, J. H., James, K., & Bauer, M. "A Learning Progression for Geometric Transformations." *ETS Research Report Series*, RR-19-01. **Verified — primary source, full text obtained and read directly.**
- **Institution/experts:** Educational Testing Service (ETS) — respected psychometric/measurement research venue, widely cited in CCSSM-aligned assessment literature.
- **Summary:** A synthesis/framework paper (not an original empirical misconception study) that explicitly builds composition into a 5-level learning progression, adapting descriptors from Soon's (1989) progression via Guven (2012). Level 3: "performs composition of simple transformations," "describes changes to states... after composite transformations," "given initial and final states, can decompose and recombine a transformation as a composition of simple transformations." Level 5: "understands associativity and commutativity and the notions of identity and inverse transformation in the context of compositions of transformations."
- **Key facts/quotes:** The report's own dedicated "Common Misconceptions" section was checked directly and **does not report any composition-specific misconception** (no commutativity-assumption error, no second-transformation-center confusion, no composed-pair-vs-net-transformation conflation is named). Misconceptions named are all single-transformation ones, e.g., "students may think that if a square with edges parallel to the coordinate axes is rotated 45°, then it is no longer a square," and (via Yanik) "some students have the misconception that circular figures cannot be translated, as there are no corners to act as 'handles.'" This absence is itself informative — the field has not yet produced a well-established, named misconception specifically for composition/sequencing. Quote: "[Students] can express a transformation as a composition of simpler transformations and can express the composed transformation as a matrix operation where the matrix is the product of the matrices..." (Level 5, p. 11 — describes a capability, not a documented error).

**5. Xistouri, Pitta-Pantazi, & Gagatsis (2014) — NOT composition-specific, flagged explicitly**
- **Citation:** Xistouri, X., Pitta-Pantazi, D., & Gagatsis, A. "Primary School Students' Structure and Levels of Abilities in Transformational Geometry." *Revista Latinoamericana de Investigación en Matemática Educativa (RELIME)*, 17(4-I), 149–164. Verified via the Fife et al. (2019) primary text describing this study's methodology/results directly, plus independent citation corroboration.
- **Institution/experts:** Not stated in raw research (peer-reviewed international journal, respected Latin American math-education venue comparable in role to a regional JRME).
- **Summary:** One of the most commonly surfaced "transformation geometry misconceptions" studies (N=166 students, Grades 4–6), but confirmed via Fife et al.'s description: the four task types (recognize image, identify transformation performed, identify parameters, construct image) were all applied to **single transformations only** — no composition task in the design. Included explicitly to rule it out.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Actual verified finding: translations are easiest, reflections/rotations harder; a student "may have a Level 3 understanding of translations but only a Level 1 understanding of reflection or rotation" (quoted via Fife et al., p. 8). **Should not be cited as evidence about composed/sequential transformations.**

**6. Yanik (2011) — NOT composition-specific, flagged explicitly**
- **Citation:** Yanik, H. B. "Prospective Middle School Mathematics Teachers' Preconceptions of Geometric Translations." *Educational Studies in Mathematics*, 78(2), 231–260. Citation independently corroborated across ERIC, Springer, ResearchGate; content well-corroborated at abstract/secondary level, full primary text not obtained.
- **Institution/experts:** Not stated in raw research (peer-reviewed journal, ESM — top-tier in the field).
- **Summary:** Widely-cited study (44 prospective teachers, clinical interviews) documenting rich single-transformation misconceptions: translation conceived as rotational vs. translational motion vs. mapping; translation vector interpreted as a force, a line of symmetry, a direction indicator, or a displacement; belief that circular figures cannot be translated ("no corners to act as 'handles'"). None of this addresses sequences of transformations or composition, per available verification.
- **Key facts/quotes:** Included because it is the most consistently surfaced "named misconception" source in this literature, and a lit review in this space will encounter it constantly — but it would be a misuse to cite it as evidence about composed transformations specifically.

**7. Yao & Manouchehri (2019)**
- **Citation:** Yao, X., & Manouchehri, A. - **Summary:** Per corroborated abstract-level descriptions (consistent across three independent search results), a 2-week constructivist teaching experiment in which middle-school students explored and generalized properties of geometric transformations in a dynamic geometry environment, with tasks explicitly including "the properties of geometric transformations and the compositions of geometric transformations." ### DOK1 — Facts

- Fife et al. (ETS): composition explicitly placed at Level 3, and commutativity/associativity/identity/inverse understanding at Level 5, of a 5-level learning progression — but the report's own misconceptions catalog contains **zero** composition-specific named misconceptions, only single-transformation ones.
- Xistouri et al.: N=166 (Grades 4-6) — verified NOT to include composition tasks; ruled out.
- Yanik: N=44 preservice teachers — verified NOT to address composition; ruled out.
- Yao & Manouchehri: composition-focused tasks confirmed to exist in the study design, but no specific findings/numbers verifiable.
- Edwards (1997): topic (composition of two reflections) confirmed, but specific error content UNVERIFIED (paywalled).

### DOK2 — Concepts

**Pattern across papers:** This sub-topic's evidence is genuinely thin, and the raw research says so explicitly rather than padding it. The clearest pattern is a *gap*, not a finding: the field has well-developed, empirically quantified misconception catalogs for single transformations (translation-vector misinterpretation, center-of-rotation confusion, rotated-shape-loses-identity, reflection-axis errors) but essentially nothing with the same rigor for composition-specific errors (order-dependence assumptions, failure to update the second transformation's reference frame/center, failure to recognize a composed pair as equivalent to a single net isometry). The one clean, quantified, composition-specific data point (Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly's 7/17) is about a conceptual/proof-context misconception ("isometry = one move"), not a computational/directional error like getting the order or angle wrong — which is a narrower kind of misconception than what a diagnoser for computed transformation *problems* (as opposed to *proofs*) would most need.

**Evidence strength, honestly assessed:** Weak-to-thin, and this should be stated plainly rather than stretched. Only one paper (Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly) offers a verified, quantified, composition-specific misconception, and it comes from N=17 preservice teachers in a proof context — not K-12 students solving computed transformation-sequence problems, which is this project's actual target population and task type. Two papers (Edwards; Yao & Manouchehri) are topically on-point by design but their specific findings are UNVERIFIED due to access limitations. The ETS learning-progression report's *absence* of any composition-specific misconception in a document explicitly built to catalog them is itself a meaningful negative finding: it suggests the field genuinely has not consolidated a named, well-measured composition misconception taxonomy, not simply that this research pass failed to find one.

**Implications for this project's design:** This is the most consequential gap for the project, and it cuts in a specific direction: there is no existing, validated taxonomy of composed-transformation misconceptions to draw misconception labels from. This means the label set for the fine-tuning project's JSON output (misconception label field) will likely need to be **constructed rather than sourced** — informed by the plausible-but-unverified error types this research repeatedly gestures at without measuring (order-dependence errors, reference-frame-not-updated-for-second-transformation errors, composed-pair-vs-single-transformation conflation per Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly) plus mechanistically-derived candidates (e.g., from the AI-failure literature in Sub-topic 1, since models exhibit analogous confusions). The project should treat its misconception taxonomy as a hypothesis to be validated against real student data collected during the project, not as an evidence-backed inventory imported from prior literature — and should say so explicitly in any methods write-up, since claiming otherwise would overstate what the raw research supports.

## Sub-topic 4: Perception-then-Reasoning Pipeline Decomposition for Diagram-Based Tasks (general evidence beyond geometry)

### Per-paper summaries

**1. DePlot**
- **Citation:** Fangyu Liu, Julian Martin Eisenschlos, Francesco Piccinno, Syrine Krichene, Chenxi Pang, Kenton Lee, Mandar Joshi, Wenhu Chen, Nigel Collier, Yasemin Altun. "DePlot: One-shot visual language reasoning by plot-to-table translation." arXiv:2212.10505 (2022; ACL Findings 2023).
- **Institution/experts:** Google DeepMind / University of Cambridge.
- **Summary:** Converts a chart image into a linearized data table via a fine-tuned image-to-text model, then hands the table to an off-the-shelf LLM (FlanPaLM/Codex) with one-shot chain-of-thought/program-of-thought prompting — no chart-specific fine-tuning of the reasoning stage at all.
- **Key facts/quotes (verified via ar5iv HTML fetch of the actual paper):** On ChartQA human-written queries (Table 5): Pix2Struct (end-to-end) 30.5%; MatCha (end-to-end SOTA at the time) 38.2%; DePlot + FlanPaLM/Codex (decomposed) 67.6% — a ~29-point jump from decomposition on the harder human-authored subset where multi-step arithmetic dominates. **2. DFE-GPS**
- **Citation:** Zeren Zhang, Jo-Ku Cheng, Jingyang Deng, Lu Tian, Jinwen Ma, Ziran Qin, Xiaokai Zhang, Na Zhu, Tuo Leng. "Diagram Formalization Enhanced Multi-Modal Geometry Problem Solver." arXiv:2409.04214 (2024).

Directly supports that end-to-end MLLM diagram perception is actively harmful, and symbolic decomposition recovers and exceeds text-only performance.

**4. TALENT**
- **Citation:** Yutong Guo (Johns Hopkins University), Wanying Wang (independent), Yue Wu (independent), Zichen Miao (Purdue University), Haoyu Wang (University at Albany). "TALENT: Table VQA via Augmented Language-Enhanced Natural-text Transcription." arXiv:2510.07098 (2025).

### DOK2 — Concepts

**Pattern across papers:** Across chart QA, geometric diagram QA, abstract/symbolic visual reasoning, and general VLM failure diagnostics, the dominant and most rigorously verified pattern is that current VLMs' raw visual *perception* is the primary bottleneck, not their downstream reasoning — and that converting the visual input into a sufficiently rich structured/symbolic text representation before reasoning consistently helps, sometimes dramatically (DePlot's 29-pt gain, Vaishnav & Tammet's 45-pt gap, VRIQ's 56%+perception-only failure attribution, MathVerse's counterintuitive vision-hurts finding, DFE-GPS's diagram-actively-decreases-accuracy finding specifically in the geometry domain). This is a genuinely broad, multi-domain, multi-lab convergence, not a single paper's idiosyncratic result.

**Evidence strength, honestly assessed:** The pro-decomposition evidence is strong and comes from several independently and directly verified sources (DePlot, DFE-GPS, MathVerse, Vaishnav & Tammet — all confirmed via direct HTML/PDF fetch with exact table numbers). However, the raw research itself surfaces genuine, well-documented counter-evidence that must not be dropped: TALENT shows that *naive* decomposition (bare OCR/markdown) can underperform strong end-to-end VLMs, and only a richer dual-representation decomposition wins; PixelWorld (UNVERIFIED specifics, but directionally consistent across multiple summaries) shows pixel-native processing beats OCR decomposition for documents/slides specifically, while still favoring decomposition for math/logic tasks. The honest synthesis is: "decompose" is not automatically a win — the intermediate representation's richness and fidelity to the task's structure is what determines whether decomposition helps or hurts, and this qualifier is essential, not a footnote.

**Implications for this project's design:** This is the most directly relevant sub-topic to the "diagram → text description → solve from text → JSON diagnosis" pipeline question, and it gives a nuanced yes: the domain-specific paper most on point (DFE-GPS) shows exactly this pattern for geometry — raw diagram input hurts, symbolic/formalized diagram input helps, on the same models and same benchmark. But the TALENT counter-evidence directly implies a design requirement: the "diagram → text" step in this project's pipeline must produce a *rich* description (e.g., preserving spatial relationships, labeled points, transformation parameters, orientation) rather than a minimal/lossy one (e.g., bare shape-and-coordinate lists that drop which point maps to which under which operation) — otherwise the pipeline risks reproducing the TALENT failure mode where decomposition actively underperforms a strong end-to-end model. Combined with Sub-topic 2's finding that even dedicated diagram parsers only reach 66.07% F1, this sub-topic's implication is that the diagram-to-text stage is simultaneously the design choice best supported by cross-domain evidence and the stage most likely to introduce silent errors if under-engineered.

## Cross-Cutting Synthesis for the Pipeline Design

**Overall verdict: partially supported, with a specific and identifiable risk concentrated in one stage.**

The proposed pipeline — diagram → text description → solve from text → JSON diagnosis — is **well-supported** in its second half (solve-from-text over solve-from-pixels) and **partially supported but risk-laden** in its first half (diagram → text conversion), specifically for the composed-transformation domain this project targets.

**What most supports the design:**
- **DFE-GPS** (Sub-topic 4) is the strongest direct support, because it is the only paper in this whole research pass that runs the exact ablation this project needs — on geometry diagrams specifically, on the same models: raw diagram input *decreases* accuracy (−3.15 to −10.85 pts across three MLLMs) while symbolic/formalized diagram input *increases* it (+4–6 pts over direct multimodal input). This is the single most on-target piece of evidence for the pipeline's premise.
- **Han et al.** and **Vaishnav & Tammet** (Sub-topics 2 and 4) generalize this beyond geometry specifically: structured/symbolic text consistently beats both raw natural-language description and raw image input, with gaps as large as ~45 points (Vaishnav & Tammet, admittedly on a synthetic benchmark) and ~37 points (Han et al., spatial join, structured features vs. plain text).
- **MathVerse** and **VRIQ** (Sub-topic 4) support the premise that perception, not reasoning, is the dominant error source in current VLMs — implying the highest-leverage place to intervene with an explicit, engineered step is exactly the diagram-parsing stage, which is what this pipeline proposes.
- **DePlot** shows that even a completely off-the-shelf LLM reasoning stage, paired with a good enough perception-to-text converter, can beat a fine-tuned end-to-end model by a wide margin — supporting the idea that this project doesn't need one monolithic model to do everything.

**What most complicates the design:**
- **PGDP5K/PGDPNet** (Sub-topic 2) is the most important complicating paper: even a dedicated, purpose-built diagram parser for plane geometry tops out at 66.07% F1. If the diagram-to-text stage for composed-transformation problems performs similarly (and there is no evidence it would do better — no paper here built or tested a parser specifically for transformation-sequence diagrams), roughly a third of diagram content will be mis-extracted before the "solve" stage ever runs, and any resulting misdiagnosis could be an artifact of parsing failure rather than a genuine reasoning or misconception signal.
- **TALENT** (Sub-topic 4) is the second key complicating paper: it demonstrates concretely that *not all decomposition is equal* — naive/lossy text extraction (bare OCR/markdown) can underperform strong end-to-end models, and only a richer, structure-preserving text representation recovers and exceeds end-to-end performance. This means the pipeline's success is conditional on engineering a sufficiently rich diagram-to-text format (e.g., explicit coordinates, labeled correspondences, transformation parameters, order) — a "just describe the picture in a sentence" converter would likely reproduce TALENT's failure mode.
- **Sub-topic 1's AI-failure literature** (Mind's Eye, DORI, TangramPuzzle, LEGO-Puzzles, OrigamiSpace) complicates the "solve from text" stage specifically for *composed* transformations: even when the input to the reasoning model is clean, models catastrophically underperform humans specifically on the composition/sequencing/ordering component of a task (e.g., TangramPuzzle's ~0% end-to-end composition success vs. 72.67% human, LEGO-Puzzles' near-zero accuracy on step-Ordering). This suggests that even after successful diagram-to-text conversion, the "solve" stage may need more than a general-purpose LLM call — possibly an explicit symbolic/coordinate-transformation solver (in the spirit of GeoDRL's or Inter-GPS's symbolic reasoning modules) rather than relying on a language model's own compositional-reasoning intuition, which this literature shows is unreliable.
- **Sub-topic 3's near-absence of a validated composition-misconception taxonomy** complicates the "misconception label" component of the JSON output specifically (not the diagram/solve stages): there is no existing, empirically-grounded inventory of composed-transformation misconceptions to draw labels from (Hegg & Fukawa-Connelly's N=17 finding about "isometry = one move" is the only verified, composition-specific, quantified misconception in the entire literature pass). The project will need to treat its misconception taxonomy as something to construct and validate empirically, not import from prior research.

**Net assessment:** The pipeline's architecture is directionally well-supported by the strongest, most directly-verified papers in this research (DFE-GPS above all, plus Han et al., Vaishnav & Tammet, MathVerse, VRIQ). But three specific risks are concrete and evidence-backed, not speculative: (1) the diagram-to-text stage itself will likely have a nontrivial error rate (PGDP5K's 66.07% F1 ceiling, achieved by a dedicated parser — this project's converter for composed-transformation diagrams specifically has no precedent to beat that number), (2) the richness of that text representation is decisive, not optional (TALENT), and (3) the "solve" stage should not assume a general LLM will compose transformations reliably even from clean text input, given the severe composition-specific degradation documented across five independent AI benchmarks in Sub-topic 1. Recommend the project budget explicit validation/QA for the diagram-to-text stage's output before it reaches the solver, and consider a symbolic/rule-based (not purely LLM-based) solving step for the composition arithmetic itself.
