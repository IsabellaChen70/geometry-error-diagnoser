# Brainlift: Fine-Tuned Small-Model Probability-Misconception Diagnoser

## Frontier-LLM Error-Detection Blind Spot

### DOK1 — Facts

- SOTA LLMs fail to reliably localize the first error in a student solution even when given the gold solution: best first-error-localization accuracy with gold solution provided was GPT-4o 63.57% (VtG) / 43.72% (PRM800K), Llama3.1-405B 62.38%/39.54% — even though the same models solve the underlying problems at 77–92% (VtG) and 70–87% (PRM800K). Source: Srivatsa, Maurya, Kochmar, "LLMs cannot spot math errors, even when allowed to peek into the solution," EMNLP 2025 Findings, arXiv:2509.01395.
- A chi-squared test in the same paper found error localization and problem-solving accuracy are weakly correlated across models: "p > 0.01; ϕ < 0.2," and feature-importance analysis found "whether the LLM solved the problem correctly (Solved?) has low importance (1.1%)." Source: same as above.
- GPT-4-Turbo scores only 48.8–51.7% on first-error-step accuracy (Task2) and a composite MR-Score of 50.5–53.0 on MR-GSM8K, despite GSM8K itself being "saturated" (SOTA >80–90%). Claude3-Haiku's first-error-step accuracy is 2.3% (k=3) despite a far higher GSM8K solve rate. Source: Zeng, Chen, Liu, Jiang, Jia, "MR-GSM8K: A Meta-Reasoning Benchmark for Large Language Model Evaluation," arXiv:2312.17080.
- UNVERIFIED: A secondary summary (emergentmind.com) claimed "top models collapse to MR-Score <0.02," but the verified GitHub results table shows lowest MR-Scores in the 4.9–23.5 range, not <0.02. Treat the <0.02 figure as unverified/incorrect.
- Complicating evidence: within-model, solving ability DOES correlate with assessment accuracy — GPT-4 showed a 42.0-point assessment-accuracy gap between solved vs. unsolved GSM8K items (χ²=39.38, p<0.001) and a 38.4-point gap on MATH (χ²=162.32, p<0.001); GPT-5 showed 34.1 and 45.9-point gaps respectively. The same paper still found assessment "more difficult than direct problem solving, especially on error-present solutions," with solve-assess gaps of 86.0–88.2 points on GSM8K. Source: Zhang, Fu, Jin, "Is Mathematical Problem-Solving Expertise in Large Language Models Associated with Assessment Performance?" arXiv:2603.25633, HCI International 2026.
- A paper on "the correct answer trap" argues AI tutors fail to flag flawed reasoning when a student's final numeric answer happens to be correct. Source: Imran, Bulathwela, "Catching The Correct Answer Trap: Characterising AI Tutor Blind Spots When Analysing Student Reasoning," arXiv:2605.23925 (2026), EEDI-sourced data. UNVERIFIED — best available signal: abstract-level framing only; specific statistics were not extractable.
- The "Bridge" dataset (700 real tutoring conversations, expert-annotated for error, remediation strategy, intention) shows LLM tutoring quality is highly sensitive to correct upstream error diagnosis: "responses from GPT-4 with expert decisions (e.g., 'simplify the problem') are +76% more preferred than without," and "random decisions decrease GPT-4's response quality by -97% [relative to] expert decisions." Source: Wang, Zhang, Robinson, Loeb, Demszky, "Bridging the Novice-Expert Gap via Models of Decision-Making," NAACL 2024, arXiv:2310.10648.
- A dataset of 1K stepwise math reasoning chains with first-error-step annotated by teachers was introduced to study step-level verification; the paper states "finding the mistake in a student solution is challenging for current models." Source: Daheim, Macina, Kapur, Gurevych, Sachan, "Stepwise Verification and Remediation of Student Reasoning Errors with Large Language Model Tutors," arXiv:2407.09136. UNVERIFIED — best available signal: qualitative difficulty claim confirmed, precise accuracy numbers not extractable.
- LLMs solve 85.5% of college algebra problems correctly for final answers, but only 56.6% of interactive tutoring dialogues are entirely correct; documented failure modes include false positives ("sub-steps are sometimes flagged as incorrect even though they are actually correct") as well as false negatives. Source: Gupta, Reddig, Calò, Weitekamp, MacLellan, "Beyond Final Answers: Evaluating Large Language Models for Math Tutoring," arXiv:2503.16460 (2025).
- GPT-4 itself commits base-rate neglect and conjunction-fallacy errors: gave the fully correct response with correct reasoning in 69.2% of relevant cases, vs. Claude 2 at 55.0%; "chain-of-thought prompting can partially mitigate, but not eliminate, probabilistic reasoning errors." Source: Macmillan-Scott, Musolesi, "(Ir)rationality and Cognitive Biases in Large Language Models," Royal Society Open Science 11(6):240255 (2024), arXiv:2402.09193.
- Complicating evidence: earlier testing found GPT-3 and GPT-4 correctly applying Bayes' theorem on the classic "blue/green cab" base-rate problem (NOT exhibiting the fallacy), but a follow-up retest of ChatGPT3.5 in August 2023 found it "provided an incorrect response and did not use Bayes theorem" — suggesting base-rate performance is version-dependent/inconsistent rather than a fixed trait. Source: Binz, Schulz, "Using Cognitive Psychology to Understand GPT-3," PNAS 120 (2023), arXiv:2206.14576.
- UNVERIFIED: a claim that "LLM evaluations on the Eedi dataset show that diagnostic accuracy degrades sharply as the number of candidate misconceptions grows" — general trend reported across secondary sources, but exact percentage drop and specific paper title/authors not pinned down.

### DOK2 — Concepts

- The core litmus test for this project is confirmed: a frontier model given the gold answer still cannot reliably localize the first error (GPT-4o 43.7–63.6%, weak ϕ<0.2 correlation with solving ability) — this validates that error/misconception diagnosis is a distinct skill from problem-solving, and that a narrow fine-tune targeting diagnosis specifically (rather than relying on a prompted frontier model) is a defensible project. Draws on: EMNLP 2025 first-error-localization stats, MR-GSM8K solve/grade gap.
- However, the "no correlation" framing should not be oversold: the within-model solved/unsolved gap (34–46 points, Zhang/Fu/Jin) shows solving ability isn't irrelevant to diagnosis — it's just insufficient. This suggests the diagnoser should be trained on genuinely erroneous solutions across a spread of difficulty, not just easy ones, since even models with strong solve rates on a topic can still misdiagnose within that topic. Draws on: MR-GSM8K solve/grade gap facts; Zhang/Fu/Jin within-model gap facts.
- The "right answer, wrong reasoning" blind spot (Gupta et al.'s 85.5% final-answer accuracy vs. 56.6% dialogue-correctness; Imran & Bulathwela's "correct answer trap") suggests the diagnoser's training data and eval harness must specifically include cases where the student's final answer is correct but the reasoning/misconception is still present (or vice versa) — a pure answer-matching filter for "is this student wrong" would systematically miss the hardest, most valuable cases. Draws on: Gupta et al. dialogue-accuracy gap; Imran & Bulathwela framing (flagged unverified stats).
- Documented false positives (flagging correct steps as incorrect) alongside false negatives means the eval harness for the diagnoser needs precision AND recall on misconception-presence, not just recall — mirrors general verifier FAR/FRR framing seen in the synthetic-data sub-topic. Draws on: Gupta et al.'s "sub-steps sometimes flagged as incorrect even though correct."
- Base-rate neglect is a genuine, literature-documented LLM weakness (Macmillan-Scott & Musolesi, 69.2% GPT-4 correct-with-reasoning rate) but is inconsistent across models/versions/prompts (Binz & Schulz's cab-problem reversal). This means the project's target misconception category (base-rate/Bayesian errors) is real and non-trivial for frontier models to reason about correctly themselves, but any baseline comparison against a frontier model must be re-run at eval time rather than assumed stable from older literature. Draws on: Macmillan-Scott & Musolesi stats; Binz & Schulz version-inconsistency finding.
- Open question / gap: no verified source establishes how diagnostic accuracy degrades as the candidate-misconception space grows (the Eedi-based claim is flagged UNVERIFIED). This is a load-bearing unknown for schema design — if scaling the misconception label set sharply hurts LLM accuracy, that argues for a smaller, curated label set in the diagnoser's fine-tune rather than mirroring Eedi's ~2,587-category catalog. Needs direct verification before committing to a taxonomy size.

## Base-Rate Neglect / Bayesian Reasoning Misconceptions (Cognitive Science)

### DOK1 — Facts

- In the original base-rate neglect study (n=60 physicians/students at Harvard Medical School), given a disease prevalence of 1/1000 and a 5% false-positive test rate, the correct Bayesian answer is ~1.96%, but the most common answer given was "95%" (27 of 60, i.e., 45%); only 11 of 60 (18%) gave the approximately correct answer. Source: Casscells, Schoenberger, Graboys, "Interpretation by Physicians of Clinical Laboratory Results," NEJM 299(18):999–1001 (1978).
- A modern replication reproduced the same pattern: "the most common answer was '95%,' given by 27 of 61 respondents (44%)," with "approximately three-quarters of respondents answered the question incorrectly (95% CI, 65% to 87%)" — 14 of 61 (23%) correct, 47 of 61 (77%) incorrect. Source: PMC4955674, "Medicine's Uncomfortable Relationship With Math: Calculating Positive Predictive Value." UNVERIFIED: full author/journal metadata for this PMC article could not be confirmed beyond the PMC ID; treat the study's existence/quoted numbers as solid but verify citation metadata before standalone use.
- In Eddy's mammography base-rate problem (1% prevalence, 80% sensitivity, ~9.6% false-positive rate), the correct Bayesian answer is 7.8%, but "95 out of 100 physicians estimated the probability of cancer given a positive test to be between 70 and 80%" — roughly 10x too high; Eddy attributed this to physicians "confus[ing] the conditional probability of breast cancer given a positive mammogram with that of a positive mammogram given breast cancer." Source: Eddy, "Probabilistic reasoning in clinical medicine," in Kahneman/Slovic/Tversky (eds.), Judgment Under Uncertainty, 1982. Note: figure is well-attested secondhand across many peer-reviewed sources but the original book chapter text could not be directly fetched.
- Kahneman & Tversky identified "insensitivity to the prior probability of outcomes" as a pervasive bias, where subjects rely on representativeness and largely ignore the base rate/prior. Source: Kahneman, Tversky, "On the Psychology of Prediction," Psychological Review 80(4):237–251 (1973).
- The classic "cab problem" (Green cabs 85%, Blue 15%, witness 80% accurate; asked P(cab was Blue | witness said Blue)) has a correct Bayesian answer of ≈41%, but most subjects answer close to 80% (the witness accuracy figure), ignoring the 85/15 base rate. This problem is most precisely sourced to Tversky & Kahneman's "Evidential Impact of Base Rates" chapter (in Judgment Under Uncertainty, 1982) rather than verbatim in the 1973 Psychological Review paper.
- "Confusion of the inverse" is the formal name for equating P(A|B) with P(B|A); it is also called "Fallacy of the Transposed Conditional," "Conditional Probability Fallacy," "conversion error," and, in legal contexts, the "Prosecutor's Fallacy" (term coined by Thompson & Schumann, 1987). Source: Villejoubert, Mandel, "The inverse fallacy," Memory & Cognition (cited via Wikipedia "Confusion of the inverse"); Thompson, Schumann, "Interpretation of Statistical Evidence in Criminal Trials," Law and Human Behavior 11(3):167–187 (1987).
- The Prosecutor's Fallacy specifically equates P(evidence | innocence) with P(innocence | evidence) — e.g., treating "1 in a million match probability" as "1-in-a-million chance of innocence," ignoring the suspect-pool base rate. Source: Thompson & Schumann (1987), as above.
- "Wrong denominator" error is a descriptive mechanism (conditioning on the wrong reference class) used widely in natural-frequency pedagogy literature but is NOT a single canonically-named term with one origin paper — flagged as a descriptive concept, not a citable named taxonomy category.
- Causal vs. incidental base rates: "A base rate is called causal if it suggests the existence of a causal factor... A base rate is called incidental if it does not." Causal base rates are used more readily by subjects; incidental base rates (e.g., disease prevalence) of equal diagnostic value are routinely ignored. Source: Tversky, Kahneman, "Evidential Impact of Base Rates," in Judgment Under Uncertainty (1982); Bar-Hillel, "The Base Rate Fallacy in Probability Judgments," Acta Psychologica 44(3):211–233 (1980). Note: Bar-Hillel's "relevance principle" terminology could not be directly verified from full text.
- When information was presented in natural-frequency format instead of single-event probabilities, "statistically naive participants derived up to 50% of inferences by Bayesian algorithms" — versus near-zero for probability-format presentation. The natural-frequency reformulation of the mammography problem ("100 out of 10,000 women... 80... will have a positive mammogram; 950 out of 9,900... will have a false-positive") makes the correct denominator (80+950=1030) visually explicit. Source: Gigerenzer, Hoffrage, "How to Improve Bayesian Reasoning Without Instruction: Frequency Formats," Psychological Review 102(4):684–704 (1995).
- Direct comparison of teaching methods: natural-frequency + visual-representation training improved performance from 10% to 90% Bayesian answers, versus an improvement from 0% to ~65% for rule-based (formula) training; natural-frequency training also showed stronger long-term retention. Source: Sedlmeier, Gigerenzer, "Teaching Bayesian Reasoning in Less Than Two Hours," Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 130(3):380–400 (2001).
- The natural-frequency effect has been "confirmed in both numerous laboratory studies and applied research, including screening for Down syndrome, the interpretation of DNA evidence in court, and teaching children to reason the Bayesian way" — i.e., not a one-off lab artifact. Source: "The Psychology of Bayesian Reasoning" review, Frontiers in Psychology (2014), PMC4191302.
- Konold's "outcome approach" misconception: individuals treat probability questions as "yes/no decisions about whether an outcome will occur on a particular trial," judged afterward as simply "right" or "wrong," rather than as long-run relative frequencies — based on interviews with 16 undergraduates. Source: Konold, "Informal Conceptions of Probability," Cognition and Instruction 6(1):59–98 (1989).
- "Equiprobability bias": "a tendency to believe that every process in which randomness is involved corresponds to a fair distribution, with equal probabilities for any possible outcome" — students assume uniform outcome likelihood by default even when told otherwise. Source: Lecoutre (1992), as reviewed in PMC4310748, "The Equiprobability Bias from a Mathematical and Psychological Perspective."
- A commonly recurring composite list of probability-misconception labels across math-ed literature includes: equiprobability bias, representativeness bias, positive/negative recency effect ("gambler's fallacy"), outcome orientation, base-rate fallacy/neglect, insensitivity to sample size, illusory correlation, belief bias, and conjunction fallacy. FLAGGED: this 8–9 item composite is the researcher's own synthesis across secondary sources, not a single canonical taxonomy with one citation — only the individual components (Konold 1989, Lecoutre 1992, and the Kahneman/Tversky trio) are independently and robustly citable.

### DOK2 — Concepts

- Base-rate neglect is one of the most rigorously replicated effects in cognitive science, spanning 45+ years and multiple domains (medical: Casscells 1978, PMC4955674 replication, Eddy 1982; legal: prosecutor's fallacy; general judgment: Kahneman & Tversky's cab problem) — this gives strong external validity to choosing base-rate/Bayesian misconceptions as the diagnoser's target domain, since the error pattern is well-characterized and stable across 4+ decades of study, not a one-off finding. Draws on: Casscells 1978, PMC4955674, Eddy 1982, Kahneman & Tversky cab problem.
- Across every base-rate study, the wrong answer is not random — it consistently defaults to a specific, predictable substitute value: the test's stated accuracy/sensitivity figure (95% in Casscells, 70-80% in Eddy, 80% in the cab problem) rather than the true posterior. This is a strong argument for designing the diagnoser's misconception taxonomy around "confusion of the inverse" as a distinct, detectable label — a model could plausibly learn to recognize "the student's stated answer equals P(evidence|hypothesis) rather than P(hypothesis|evidence)" as a specific, checkable pattern rather than a vague "got it wrong" bucket. Draws on: Casscells/PMC4955674 "95%" answer pattern, Eddy's 70-80% pattern, cab problem's ~80% pattern, and the formal "confusion of the inverse" / prosecutor's fallacy definitions.
- The natural-frequency vs. probability-format contrast (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage: near-zero vs. up to 50% correct; Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer: 10%→90% vs. 0%→65%) is the single most actionable pedagogical finding for this project: it suggests that synthetic training examples for the diagnoser should include BOTH probability-format and natural-frequency-format problem framings, since misconception patterns likely differ by framing, and a diagnoser trained only on one framing may not generalize. It also argues for a specific taxonomy entry like "denominator-neglect" or "framing-sensitive base-rate error" tied to whether the student was given frequencies vs. probabilities. Draws on: Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995, Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer 2001.
- The causal-vs-incidental base rate distinction (Tversky & Kahneman 1982, Bar-Hillel 1980) suggests a second orthogonal taxonomy axis beyond "did the student neglect the base rate": whether the problem's base rate is causally-framed (more likely to be used correctly) or incidental (more likely to be ignored) — this could be a generation-time control variable for synthetic data, letting the project test whether the diagnoser's accuracy depends on this framing.
- Equiprobability bias (Lecoutre) and the outcome approach (Konold) are well-established, independently-citable misconceptions that are distinct from base-rate neglect (they concern default uniform-probability assumptions and deterministic right/wrong framing of chance, respectively, not prior-updating). This confirms the target taxonomy should treat these as separate labels from base-rate neglect/confusion-of-the-inverse rather than lumping all "probability errors" into one bucket — supporting a multi-class (not binary) misconception schema.
- Open question / gap: there is no single canonically-sourced, complete taxonomy of probability misconceptions — the widely-cited 8-9 item composite list is the researcher's own synthesis, not one peer-reviewed source. This means the project's misconception label schema itself will need to be assembled by combining several independently-verified sources (Konold 1989, Lecoutre 1992, Kahneman & Tversky base-rate/representativeness/conjunction work, confusion-of-the-inverse/prosecutor's fallacy) rather than adopted wholesale from an existing published taxonomy — a design decision to make explicitly rather than defer.

## Synthetic Data Generation + Auto-Verification Pipelines for Fine-Tuning

### DOK1 — Facts

- Self-Instruct filtered generated instructions using "ROUGE-L similarity with any existing instruction is less than 0.7" as a deduplication threshold, plus removal of unprocessable/duplicate instructions; final released dataset was 52K instructions and 82K+ instance pairs. Source: Wang et al., "Self-Instruct: Aligning Language Models with Self-Generated Instructions," ACL 2023, arXiv:2212.10560.
- Alpaca's data generation (52K unique instructions + outputs, seeded from 175 human-written pairs, generated via text-davinci-003) "costed less than $500 using the OpenAI API." Source: Taori et al., Stanford CRFM Alpaca blog, 2023.
- STaR's "rationalization" technique plants the ground-truth answer as a hint during generation, then strips the hint before using the rationale as a training example: "we do not include the hint in its corresponding prompt, as if the model had come up with the rationale without the hint." On CommonsenseQA, rationalization improved accuracy from 68.8% to 72.5% (+3.7 pts) vs. rejection-sampling-only; on 2-digit arithmetic, rationalization took accuracy from <1% to 32% after one iteration; human raters preferred STaR rationales over few-shot rationales 30% more often. Source: Zelikman et al., "STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning," NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2203.14465.
- Constitutional AI's supervised stage generates self-critiques and revisions and fine-tunes on the revised responses; its RL stage samples pairs, uses a model to judge which is better, and trains a preference model as reward signal (RLAIF). Source: Bai et al., "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," Anthropic, 2022, arXiv:2212.08073.
- Llama 2's rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT) samples K candidates per prompt, scores with a reward model, and keeps the best (best-of-K) for SFT; only the 70B model does rejection sampling directly, and smaller models are fine-tuned on rejection-sampled data from the larger model; each of 5 successive RLHF rounds pools the best samples from the current AND all prior iterations (pooling only the latest iteration caused regressions). Source: Touvron et al., "Llama 2," Meta, 2023, arXiv:2307.09288.
- Orca 1 sampled 5M instructions from FLAN, obtained ChatGPT responses for all 5M and GPT-4 responses for a 1M subset, training progressively (ChatGPT traces first, then GPT-4 traces); stated rationale is that with plain imitation learning "student models mostly learn to imitate the teacher model's style, rather than its reasoning process." Orca 2 adds "Cautious Reasoning": the teacher is given a strategy hint which is erased before the student sees the example. Source: Mukherjee et al., "Orca," Microsoft Research, arXiv:2306.02707; Mitra et al., "Orca 2," 2023.
- Phi-1's pipeline used GPT-4 to annotate "educational value" quality on ~100k samples, then trained a classifier to filter the rest; the source corpus (The Stack + StackOverflow) was "over 35 million files/samples, totalling over 35B tokens," filtered down to "about 6B tokens" (~83% reduction). The filtered-subset model reached 17.68% HumanEval pass@1 vs. 12.19% for the same-size model trained on the full unfiltered corpus. Source: Gunasekar et al., "Textbooks Are All You Need," Microsoft Research, 2023, arXiv:2306.11644.
- ReverseMath's verifier reports per-benchmark acceptance rates: GSM8K 96.0% (96/100), MGSM 100.0% (1100/1100), MATH-500 100.0% (100/100), AgentCoMa 97.8% (176/180), AIME2024 93.3% (28/30), AIME2025 93.3% (28/30). Source: "ReverseMath: Answer Inversion for Scalable and Verifiable Mathematical Problem Generation," arXiv:2605.27709 (2026). UNVERIFIED — publication date/arXiv ID falls after typical knowledge-cutoff cross-checking ability and figures were retrieved via a single WebFetch pass without independent corroboration.
- Code-generation verifier literature defines False Acceptance Rate (FAR, wrongly accepting an incorrect solution) and False Rejection Rate (FRR, wrongly rejecting a correct one) as standard verifier-quality metrics; richer test suites reduce FAR and improve downstream pass@1, while "quantity alone yields diminishing returns" once verification quality is the bottleneck. Source: "Dynamic Scaling of Unit Tests for Code Reward Modeling," arXiv:2501.01054; "Verification Limits Code LLM Training," arXiv:2509.20837. UNVERIFIED — specific FAR/FRR percentages not extracted, only qualitative framing confirmed.
- LIMA fine-tuned a 65B LLaMA model on just 1,000 curated prompt/response pairs (750 from Stack Exchange/wikiHow + 250 hand-written), producing outputs "equivalent or strictly preferred to GPT-4 in 43% of cases," 58% vs. Bard, 65% vs. DaVinci003 in blind human evaluation. This supports the "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis": "A model's knowledge and capabilities are learnt almost entirely during pretraining, while alignment teaches it which subdistribution of formats should be used." Source: Zhou et al., "LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment," 2023, arXiv:2305.11206. Note: this is human-curated data, not automated-verifier-filtered data.
- MetaMath's "Answer Augmentation" generates multiple reasoning paths and keeps only those whose final answer matches ground truth (a post-hoc filter); its "backward reasoning" augmentation and ReverseMath's "answer inversion" instead construct the problem statement around a pre-chosen answer, making the answer "known by construction" before a secondary verifier checks for degenerate cases (leakage, non-unique answers). Source: Yu et al., "MetaMath," arXiv:2309.12284 (quotes paraphrased/approximate, not independently refetched); ReverseMath, arXiv:2605.27709 (flagged above).
- No paper was found reporting a literal "~75% yield rate" for verifier-filtered synthetic data generation matching that specific framing; closest analogues are Phi-1's implicit ~17% token retention (6B kept of 35B) and ReverseMath's ~93–100% per-benchmark acceptance rates — a wide range that should not be collapsed into a single "75%" figure.

### DOK2 — Concepts

- Two fundamentally different verifier-filtering strategies appear across the precedent set: (1) post-hoc rejection sampling on freely-generated content (Self-Instruct's ROUGE-L dedup, Llama 2's best-of-K reward filtering, MetaMath's answer-matching filter, Phi-1's GPT-4-as-classifier), and (2) construction-time ground-truth planting where the label is embedded before generation and a lightweight verifier only catches degenerate edge cases (STaR's rationalization, MetaMath's backward reasoning, ReverseMath's answer inversion). For a probability-misconception diagnoser, strategy (2) is likely more efficient and higher-yield (ReverseMath's 93-100% vs. Phi-1's implicit ~17%) because the "ground truth" (which misconception was injected) can be planted at generation time rather than inferred after the fact. Draws on: STaR rationalization mechanism, ReverseMath acceptance rates, Phi-1 retention rate, MetaMath answer augmentation.
- The Phi-1 and LIMA results together establish a strong "quality/curation beats raw quantity" pattern for small-model fine-tuning specifically (17.68% vs 12.19% HumanEval on 6B vs 35B tokens; 1K curated examples rivaling large-scale RLHF): this directly supports investing in a smaller, verifier-filtered or hand-curated synthetic dataset for the diagnoser rather than maximizing raw example count. Draws on: Phi-1 filtered-vs-unfiltered HumanEval delta; LIMA's 1,000-example result.
- STaR's rationalization technique (plant the answer, generate the rationale, then strip the hint) is a directly transferable recipe for this project: generate synthetic "student solutions containing misconception X" by telling the teacher model which misconception to inject, then stripping that instruction from the final training example so the diagnoser must infer the misconception from the surface solution alone, exactly as it will need to at inference time. Draws on: STaR's hint-then-strip mechanism and its accuracy gains from rationalization vs. rejection-sampling-only.
- Constitutional AI's and Orca's self-critique/explanation-tuning patterns suggest a viable "verifier as second LLM call" design: after a teacher model generates a synthetic misconception-labeled example, a second (possibly the same) model can be prompted to check whether the generated student solution actually and unambiguously exhibits the labeled misconception (an intermediate step BFCL-style AST/semantic check does not cover) — reducing false-labeled synthetic examples before they reach the fine-tuning set. Draws on: Constitutional AI's critique-then-revise stage; Orca's teacher-hint-then-erase pattern.
- Open question / gap: no verified precedent gives a concrete expected "yield rate" (fraction of generated examples that survive verification) for a task structurally similar to this project's synthetic generation (student solution + injected misconception + probability-value fields). The two extremes found (Phi-1 ~17% token retention vs. ReverseMath 93-100% per-item acceptance) bound a very wide range and should not be used to set a target yield without a pilot run — this is a load-bearing unknown for planning the synthetic-generation budget.
- The RFT/Llama 2 "pool across iterations, not just the latest" finding (pooling only latest-iteration best samples caused regressions) is a concrete, transferable hyperparameter-style decision if the project does multiple rounds of synthetic generation + fine-tuning: retain a cumulative pool of previously-verified good examples rather than replacing the training set each round. Draws on: Llama 2 RFT's iterative pooling fact.

## QLoRA / Unsloth Small-Model Fine-Tuning Facts

### DOK1 — Facts

- QLoRA reduces memory enough to "finetune a 65B parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance," versus "more than 780 GB of GPU memory" for regular 16-bit finetuning of the same model. Source: Dettmers, Pagnoni, Holtzman, Zettlemoyer, "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs," arXiv:2305.14314 (2023).
- Per-size QLoRA memory footprints: 33B model ~21GB (vs. 66GB 16-bit baseline); 13B ~10GB (vs. 26GB baseline); 7B 5–6GB (vs. ~26GB baseline). Double Quantization reduces quantization-constant overhead from 0.5 bits/parameter to 0.127 bits/parameter (~3GB saved on a 65B model). Source: same as above.
- QLoRA's Guanaco model family reached "99.3% of the performance level of ChatGPT" on the Vicuna benchmark with "only 24 hours of finetuning on a single [consumer] GPU," and "outperforms all previous openly released models on the Vicuna benchmark." Source: same as above. Flag: this figure is based on GPT-4-as-judge pairwise comparison on one benchmark — a self-reported, single-methodology result, not broadly externally validated.
- Unsloth's README claims: "Train and RL 500+ models up to 2x faster with up to 70% less VRAM"; specific entries include gpt-oss (20B): "2x faster, 70% less memory"; Qwen3.5 GSPO: "2x faster, 70% less memory"; MoE LLM training: "12x faster with 35% less VRAM"; GRPO/RL: "80% less VRAM." Supported base models include Llama, Qwen (3/3.5/3.6), Mistral, Gemma, DeepSeek, gpt-oss, Phi-4, GLM. Source: unslothai/unsloth GitHub README, 2024-2026.
- UNVERIFIED counter-claim: a 2026 arXiv paper "Chronicals: A High-Performance Framework for LLM Fine-Tuning with 3.51x Speedup over Unsloth" (arXiv:2601.02609) reportedly questions some of Unsloth's benchmark comparisons; the specific critique could not be fully verified in this research pass.
- Qwen3 released six open-weight dense models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B, 32B) under Apache 2.0, plus two MoE models (30B-A3B, 235B-A22B); smallest dense models ship with 32K context, larger dense/MoE variants support 128K; all models feature switchable "thinking"/"non-thinking" modes. ## Eval Methodology: LLM-as-Judge, Structured-Output Eval, Held-Out Harnesses

### DOK1 — Facts

- "Strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans." The study also studies "position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases, as well as limited reasoning ability" as core judge limitations, and built MT-Bench (releasing "MT-bench questions, 3K expert votes, and 30K conversations with human preferences"). Source: Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena," NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2306.05685.
- UNVERIFIED: a commonly-repeated secondary-source figure claims GPT-4↔human agreement is ~85% vs. ~81% human-human agreement; the primary abstract only confirms "over 80%," so treat the specific 85%/81% split as plausible but not verified verbatim.
- "LLM evaluators assign higher ratings to texts with lower perplexity, regardless of whether the text was self-generated or produced by other models" — the paper's hypothesis is "the essence of the [self-preference] bias lies in perplexity" rather than literal self-recognition; GPT-4 was found to "exhibit a significant degree of self-preference bias." Source: Wataoka, Takahashi, Ri, "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2410.21819 (2024).
- Judges "prefer a semantically equivalent but more verbose response," and multimodal LLM judges are "even more vulnerable to verbosity bias than to position bias." Reported mitigation practice: randomize answer order across two passes, average over permutations, add explicit anti-verbosity instructions/rubric weight. Source: "Justice or Prejudice? Quantifying Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2410.02736 (2024).
- BFCL uses a two-tier grading scheme: "AST evaluation parses the generated call into a syntax tree and checks that the function name, required parameters, values, and types match the ground truth, ignoring argument order," plus "Execution Checking" where "candidate calls are executed in sandboxed environments; runtime errors lead to rejection." The benchmark spans "over 2,000 question-function-answer pairs" across Python, Java, JavaScript, REST. Source: Patil, Mao, et al., "The Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL)," ICML 2025.
- JSONSchemaBench tested six constrained-decoding frameworks (Guidance, Outlines, Llamacpp, XGrammar, OpenAI, Gemini) across "10K real-world JSON schemas"; compliance varies widely by framework/schema complexity (e.g., paraphrased: "Guidance achieves 92-98% compliance, while Outlines demonstrates only 6-96% depending on dataset complexity" — flagged as approximate, not verbatim). Core stated takeaway: schema-valid JSON and semantically/factually correct field values are separate axes — "methods address only syntactic validity, not whether the extracted values are correct." Source: Geng et al., "Generating Structured Outputs from Language Models: Benchmark and Studies," arXiv:2501.10868 (2025).
- UNVERIFIED: "StructEval" reportedly finds "even o1-mini reaches only a 75.58 average score" across 18 structured formats/44 task types — this number could not be independently cross-checked against a primary source. The directionally-supported, higher-confidence finding from the same cluster is "schema compliance is high, but correct field values remain much harder." Source: "The Structured Output Benchmark," arXiv:2604.25359 (2026, date/venue unverified).
- PasswordEval "measures whether language models can correctly determine when a user request is authorized (i.e., with a correct password)... we find that reasoning traces frequently leak confidential information, which calls into question whether reasoning traces should be exposed to users." It scores leakage separately in reasoning/scratchpad traces vs. final answers, plus a utility score for correct reveal when authorized. Source: Sam, Robey, Zou, Fredrikson, Kolter, "Evaluating Language Model Reasoning about Confidential Information," arXiv:2508.19980 (2025). Flag: exact numeric leak-rate percentages not retrievable in this pass — only the qualitative "reasoning traces leak more than final answers" claim is confirmed verbatim.
- "All examined models leaked third-party information during note generation, even under privacy-oriented instructions" in a clinical-documentation privacy study. Source: PMC13076117, "Evaluating privacy leakages in LLM-driven ambient clinical documentation."
- Benchmark leakage is generally defined as anything that "allows a model to 'cheat' by using information that it should not have access to" — a framing distinction between train/test contamination leakage and instructed-information leakage. Source: "A Survey on Large Language Model Benchmarks," arXiv:2508.15361 (2025).
- Standard practitioner guidance recommends measuring the fine-tuned model against the base model "on the same held-out test set" to get "a clear before-and-after picture," with a common rule of thumb of "running the base model on 50-100 representative examples" before fine-tuning to establish "a concrete performance number to improve upon." Task-specific metrics recommended alongside pass/fail JSON validity include "accuracy for classification, BLEU for machine translation, and ROUGE for summarization." Source: Databricks "A Practical Guide to LLM Fine Tuning"; Stanford Research Computing "Fine-Tuning Open Source Models" blog. Flag: aggregated/paraphrased practitioner guidance, not a peer-reviewed methodology paper; "50-100 examples" is a rule of thumb, not empirically derived.

### DOK2 — Concepts

- The BFCL AST+execution two-tier pattern and JSONSchemaBench's syntactic-vs-semantic separation both point to the same design principle for this project's eval harness: schema/parse validity (does the JSON parse, are keys/types correct) and content correctness (is the misconception label actually right) must be scored as two independent metrics, not conflated into one pass/fail — a model can be 100% schema-valid while still being semantically wrong about the misconception. Draws on: BFCL's AST/execution split; JSONSchemaBench's syntactic-vs-value-correctness finding; StructEval's "schema compliance high, field values harder" pattern (flagged partially unverified).
- LLM-as-judge is a viable grading strategy for the free-text portions of this project's eval (e.g., grading rationale quality) given the "over 80% agreement" finding, but the documented verbosity and self-preference biases (Wataoka et al.'s perplexity-based self-preference; the verbosity-bias paper) mean the judge prompt must explicitly control for response length and avoid using the same model family as both generator and judge wherever possible — otherwise the judge may systematically favor either overly verbose or stylistically-familiar diagnoser outputs regardless of correctness. Draws on: Zheng et al.'s bias taxonomy; Wataoka et al.'s perplexity finding; verbosity-bias paper's mitigation practices.
- PasswordEval's leak-scoring methodology (separately scoring reasoning-trace leakage vs. final-answer leakage, paired with a utility score) is directly transferable if the diagnoser has any free-text/scratchpad field alongside its strict-JSON output — the project should score "did the model reveal information it shouldn't have" (e.g., internal-only fields, if any) separately from "did the model still produce a usable diagnosis," mirroring PasswordEval's separation of leak-rate from utility. Draws on: PasswordEval's dual-scoring design; the clinical-documentation finding that leakage persists "even under privacy-oriented instructions" (suggesting a baseline non-zero leak rate should be expected and tested for, not assumed to be zero).
- The practitioner consensus on held-out evaluation (same test set for base vs. fine-tuned model, ~50-100 baseline examples) gives a concrete, low-cost starting methodology for this project's base-vs-fine-tuned delta reporting, though the "50-100" figure is a rule of thumb rather than a validated minimum — the project should treat this as a floor and consider a larger held-out set given that the misconception taxonomy likely has more than a handful of classes needing individual representation. Draws on: Databricks/Stanford practitioner guidance.
- Open question / gap: several of the most attention-grabbing numbers in this sub-topic (StructEval's 75.58 o1-mini score, JSONSchemaBench's exact Guidance/Outlines percentages, PasswordEval's leak-rate percentages, the 85%/81% judge-agreement split) are flagged UNVERIFIED or paraphrased — none should be cited as a precise benchmark target in the project's own methodology section without direct primary-source verification first.

## Existing Math-Tutoring Error-Diagnosis Datasets/Benchmarks

### DOK1 — Facts

- MathDial contains ~3,000 one-to-one tutor-student dialogues grounded in GSM8K word problems; students are simulated by GPT-3.5 prompted to hold a "confusion," tutors are crowdworkers; format is multi-turn dialogue transcripts with metadata (problem, ground-truth solution, free-text student confusion/error description), with no fixed closed-set misconception taxonomy and no probability/Bayesian content (grounded entirely in GSM8K arithmetic/algebra). Source: Macina, Daheim, Chowdhury, Sinha, Kapur, Gurevych, Sachan, "MathDial," EMNLP Findings 2023, arXiv:2305.14536.
- The Bridge dataset contains 700 real online tutoring conversation snippets (novice tutor response + expert revision), annotated with three expert-modeled fields: error type, remediation strategy, and intention — a multi-field, non-single-label schema, with no confirmed fixed closed-set taxonomy and no confirmed probability-domain coverage. Source: Wang et al., "Bridging the Novice-Expert Gap via Models of Decision-Making," NAACL 2024; github.com/rosewang2008/bridge.
- The EEDI / NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge dataset comprises ~125k students, ~28k questions, ~20M answer records (Eedi platform, Sept 2018-May 2020); each item is a 4-option MCQ with distractors engineered to embody specific misconceptions, tagged with a 4-level curriculum hierarchy (subject → topic → subtopic → construct). It is response-log data for predictive-modeling tasks, not a per-item strict-JSON single-misconception-label schema; whether "probability" appears in the topic taxonomy is UNVERIFIED (full curriculum taxonomy list not accessed). Source: Wang, Lamb, Saveliev et al., arXiv:2007.12061 and arXiv:2104.04034 (PMLR v133, NeurIPS Competition Track).
- The Eedi "Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics" Kaggle 2024 / "MAP" Kaggle 2025 competitions use 1,857 K-12 math questions with 4 expert-written MCQ options each, mapped to ~2,587 unique misconception categories (with text descriptions covering 4,338 of 7,428 total answer choices per the MISTAKE paper's re-description, arXiv:2510.11502); MAP frames this as a ranking task scored by MAP@3; the competition drew 1,850+ teams, ~40,000 submissions, top scores >0.94 MAP@3. The label set is a large open catalog tied to a specific item bank, output is a ranked top-3 list (not single strict-JSON label), and it is single-answer MCQ, not paired/joint-probability structured. Whether probability/Bayes-specific misconception categories exist in the full ~2,587-item list is UNVERIFIED (not exhaustively inspected). Source: Kaggle competition pages; MISTAKE paper arXiv:2510.11502; case study at the-learning-agency.com.
- MalAlgoQA is a real-world MCQ dataset (grades 3-11; math subset 807 questions, reading subset 290 questions) where each incorrect answer choice has an associated "malgorithm" (explicit flawed reasoning process); task is "Malgorithm Identification," scored via Malgorithm/Algorithm Identification Accuracy. It is structured as MCQ-with-rationale-set (choice-selection accuracy), not strict single-JSON output with a fixed misconception vocabulary; no evidence found of probability/Bayes content in the math subset. Source: "MalAlgoQA," arXiv:2407.00938 (2024).
- PRM800K contains 800,000 step-level human correctness labels over LLM-generated MATH-dataset solutions, in JSON-lines format with a `label.steps[]` array rating each step candidate on a ternary scale (-1/0/+1) plus a `finish_reason` field (`found_error`, `solution`, `bad_problem`, `give_up`). This is step-level correctness scoring, not misconception classification — no descriptive label names why a step is wrong. MATH (the underlying dataset) includes a "Counting & Probability" subject category per Hendrycks et al. 2021 (arXiv:2103.03874), so some underlying problems are probability-flavored, but PRM800K applies no probability-specific or misconception-specific labels. Source: Lightman, Kosaraju, Burda, et al. (OpenAI), "Let's Verify Step by Step," 2023; github.com/openai/prm800k.
- Two distinct, similarly-named "VerifyBench" papers exist: (A) "Benchmarking Reference-based Reward Systems for LLMs," arXiv:2505.15801, curated from 41 open-source reasoning datasets, evaluating whether a verifier correctly judges if a candidate answer matches a reference; (B) "A Systematic Benchmark for Evaluating Reasoning Verifiers Across Domains," arXiv:2507.09884, ~4,000 expert-level questions across math/physics/chemistry/biology, ~3,989 annotated instances, binary correct/incorrect label distribution ~45%/55%, inter-annotator agreement 0.88-0.92. Both are binary correctness-verification benchmarks with no error taxonomy and no probability-specific subsetting confirmed. Flag: could not fully verify whether these two same-named 2025 papers are independent or related efforts.
- BEA 2025's MRBench pools curated dialogue excerpts from MathDial and Bridge: 300 dev dialogues (2,476 responses) + 191 test dialogues (1,547 responses); four tracks each use a 3-way categorical label (Yes/To some extent/No): Mistake Identification, Mistake Location, Providing Guidance, Actionability; a 5th track (Tutor Identification) is 9-class. Explicitly stated as "focused on the mathematical domain only," inheriting MathDial (GSM8K-based) and Bridge content, with no evidence of probability-specific content. Labels evaluate tutor-response quality, not student-misconception identity. Source: Kochmar, Maurya, Petukhova, Srivatsa, Tack, Vasselli, "Findings of the BEA 2025 Shared Task on Pedagogical Ability Assessment of AI-powered Tutors," arXiv:2507.10579; MRBench introduction arXiv:2412.09416.
- A GSM8K error-typology taxonomy (9 categories: Calculation, Counting, Context Value, Contradictory Step, Missing Step, Hallucination, Unit Conversion, Operator, Formula Confusion) and a separate 6-category scheme recur in secondary literature, applied post-hoc to GSM8K model outputs (generic error-type, not misconception-content taxonomies); no probability content since GSM8K is elementary arithmetic. FLAGGED: primary paper for the 9-category taxonomy could not be pinned down with full citation confidence — treat as weakly/uncertainly sourced.
- Cognitive-bias/base-rate-fallacy LLM benchmarks exist adjacent to this space (e.g., "CogBias," arXiv:2604.01366, 2026; a "BRU" dataset; "Martingale Score," arXiv:2512.02914) but test LLMs-as-subjects on classic psychology vignettes, not K-12 students, and are not framed as misconception-classification tasks with a JSON schema. Flag: these are very recent (2025/2026) papers with arXiv IDs at the edge of typical indexing lag; exact dataset sizes/schemas not independently verified beyond search snippets.
- A fetched secondary source fabricated an incorrect backronym expansion of "EEDI" ("Educational Endpoint for Data Intelligence") — this is a hallucination, discarded; "Eedi" is simply a UK ed-tech company/platform brand name, not a documented acronym in any primary source found.

### DOK2 — Concepts

- Across all ten lines of prior art reviewed (MathDial, Bridge, EEDI/NeurIPS 2020, Eedi/MAP Kaggle, MalAlgoQA, PRM800K, VerifyBench x2, BEA/MRBench, GSM8K taxonomies, cognitive-bias vignette suites), no dataset combines all three of: (a) strict machine-parseable JSON output schema, (b) a single closed-set misconception label per instance, and (c) a problem domain specifically structured around two-event/Bayesian probability computations — this directly supports the project's novelty claim, though it rests on a synthesis across many partially-verified sources rather than one definitive negative-result search. Draws on: all ten dataset facts above, especially the "difference from target format" notes on each.
- The existing large misconception catalogs (Eedi/MAP's ~2,587 categories) use ranked top-k output (MAP@3) rather than single-label strict JSON — this suggests that if this project's taxonomy grows large, a ranked/top-k output format may be a more realistic target than a single best-guess label, and the project should decide early whether it wants Eedi-style breadth (thousands of item-specific misconceptions) or a small curated set (as suggested by the base-rate-neglect cognitive-science sub-topic's ~8-10 named categories). Draws on: Eedi/MAP scale and MAP@3 framing; cross-reference to the base-rate-neglect sub-topic's composite taxonomy gap.
- PRM800K and the VerifyBench pair establish that "is this correct" (binary/ternary correctness verification) is a well-trodden, separately-solved problem from "why is this wrong" (misconception naming) — no existing benchmark fuses the two. This means the diagnoser's task (naming the specific misconception) is a genuinely different and harder task than what most existing verifier/reward-model infrastructure was built for, reinforcing that off-the-shelf verifiers (PRM-style) cannot be repurposed directly as the diagnoser without a labeled misconception layer. Draws on: PRM800K's ternary correctness-only schema; VerifyBench A/B's binary correctness framing.
- MRBench's three-way ordinal labels (Yes/To some extent/No) across four tutor-quality dimensions is a useful precedent for how to score partial credit in this project's eval, distinct from a strict single-label accuracy metric — if the diagnoser sometimes identifies a "related but not exact" misconception, an ordinal or partial-credit scoring scheme (rather than strict exact-match) may better reflect real diagnostic usefulness. Draws on: MRBench's 3-way categorical label design.
- Open question / gap (explicitly flagged by the source research itself): the full ~2,587-item Eedi/MAP misconception catalog was not exhaustively audited for a hidden probability/Bayes subset, and the EEDI/NeurIPS topic taxonomy's probability coverage is unverified — both should be checked directly against underlying data files before formally asserting the novelty claim in any write-up, since finding an existing probability-specific misconception subset in Eedi's data would partially weaken the novelty argument. Draws on: EEDI/NeurIPS and Eedi/MAP "Bayes/probability coverage: unverified" flags.

Draws on: the GSM8K taxonomy fact's low-confidence flag.
